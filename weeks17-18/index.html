Weeks 17-18: WMS, process flow and impacts

Build an evidence-based WMS and production flowchart, then explain social and environmental trade-offs honestly.

Theory 1

Building a real Pen Work Method Statement

A Work Method Statement (WMS) for the Pen project is a practical plan that explains how the manufacture will be completed safely and in the correct sequence. It should show the real stages of this project, not a generic list that could belong to any workshop task. The assessment requires students to sequence Pen manufacture and identify the hazards and controls connected with that sequence. A useful WMS therefore links each task step to what could cause harm, how harm might occur, and which approved controls must be in place before work continues. The supplied Pen Making resource, teacher directions and current school SOP or SWMS remain authoritative.

Task-step specificity means breaking the project into meaningful stages that can actually be observed and checked. Broad entries such as 'make the Pen safely' are too vague to guide action or prove understanding. A stronger WMS names a stage such as inspecting the timber, marking orientation references, preparing the drilled parts, arranging the approved mandrel, turning, surface preparation, finishing or assembly. The student then records the hazard and control relevant to that particular stage. The WMS should follow the approved project sequence, but it must not add invented machine settings, component details or procedures that are absent from the kit information and teacher demonstration.

Hazard–event–consequence thinking makes each entry clearer. The hazard is the source of possible harm, the event describes what could happen, and the consequence explains the injury, damage or exposure that might result. For example, rotating equipment is a hazard; a loose item becoming caught is the event; and harm to the user could be the consequence. The control column should then state the approved measures that prevent the event or reduce its effect. This structure is more useful than writing isolated words such as 'lathe — dangerous' because it demonstrates cause and effect and helps the student choose controls that match the real risk.

The hierarchy of controls helps students select stronger measures before relying on PPE. Where practical, a hazard may be removed, separated or controlled through approved equipment, workholding, guards, isolation, teacher authorisation or another higher-level measure stated in the local procedure. Administrative controls include the WMS itself, the teacher demonstration, checkpoints, signage and the current SOP or SWMS. Task-specific PPE may also be required, but the exact requirements come from the teacher and local documents. Students must not invent a PPE combination or treat PPE as the only control. A complete entry shows how several approved controls work together.

Residual risk is the risk that remains after approved controls have been applied. It reminds students that controls reduce risk but do not make careless behaviour acceptable. The student should consider whether the remaining risk is acceptable under teacher supervision and whether authorisation is required before the stage begins. If the task, equipment, material, product or workshop condition changes, the WMS must be reviewed. A changed situation may introduce a new hazard or make an existing control unsuitable. Students stop, report the change and update the relevant entry with teacher guidance rather than continuing under an outdated plan.

The finished WMS should be an answerable evidence task. For each Pen stage, the student records the task, hazard, possible event and consequence, approved controls, teacher checkpoint where required, and any review prompted by change. Evidence can include the completed WMS, dated teacher checks, and photographs or notes showing that the listed controls were actually used. A strong reflection compares the plan with what occurred: which controls worked, what changed, and what was revised. This proves that the WMS guided real decisions during manufacture instead of being completed after the Pen was finished merely to satisfy paperwork.

  • Write the Pen stages in the approved manufacturing sequence.
  • For each stage, identify the hazard, possible event and likely consequence.
  • Select the strongest practical controls from teacher directions and local procedures.
  • Record residual risk and the teacher checkpoint required before proceeding.
  • Review and update the WMS whenever the task or conditions change.
Key idea: A real Pen WMS connects each approved task step to specific hazards, possible consequences, effective controls, teacher authorisation and evidence that the plan was followed and reviewed.

Theory 2

Creating the Pen production flowchart: inputs, processes and outputs

A Pen production flowchart is a visual explanation of how resources are transformed through an ordered series of processes into intended and unintended outputs. The assessment requires the flowchart to identify inputs, processes and outputs, while also allowing students to connect relevant social and environmental impacts. It should represent the approved Pen project accurately rather than filling gaps with guessed construction details. Begin with the supplied Pen Making resource, the kit instructions, teacher demonstrations and the project report requirements. These sources provide the stages and information that can be included, while any missing detail must be confirmed with the teacher before it appears in the chart.

Inputs are everything required before or during production. Material inputs include the approved timber and supplied Pen kit. Information inputs include the project brief, cutting list, Work Method Statement, kit resource, product information, teacher directions and workshop SOP or SWMS. Human inputs include student skill, teacher supervision, observation and decision-making. Energy inputs may include the energy required for teacher-approved workshop equipment, lighting and dust-control systems, but students should not invent machine settings or claim equipment that has not been confirmed locally. Recording several kinds of input shows that manufacturing depends on more than raw material alone.

Processes are the approved actions that transform the inputs. The flowchart should place these stages in a logical sequence, using clear action labels rather than long paragraphs. Relevant stages may include timber selection and inspection, reference marking, approved preparation of the two parts, brass sleeve preparation, teacher-approved mandrel staging, turning, surface inspection, the approved abrasive sequence, finishing, component staging and assembly. These labels describe recognised project stages without adding unverified dimensions, tools or methods. Arrows should show how one completed stage leads to the next and where a teacher checkpoint is required before the process can continue.

A useful production flowchart also includes decisions, inspections and feedback loops. Production is rarely a perfectly straight line because a quality check may show that work is not ready for the next stage. A decision point might ask whether the timber is suitable, whether grain references correspond, whether the developing surface meets the approved criterion or whether teacher approval has been obtained. A â€˜no’ result should lead back to an approved inspection, correction or consultation stage rather than to an improvised fix. A â€˜yes’ result allows the sequence to continue. These loops show that quality is created through repeated check–decide–act cycles, not through one uninterrupted run from material to finished Pen.

Outputs include more than the intended timber Pen. Intended outputs may include the completed Pen, the project report, photographs, quality records and evaluation evidence. Other outputs may include usable offcuts, timber dust, swarf, packaging and other waste or by-products generated by the approved process. Students should record only outputs that genuinely apply to their local project. The flowchart can then connect these outputs to environmental impacts such as material use, waste generation and energy demand, and to social impacts such as safe working conditions, responsible use of shared equipment, product usefulness and the development of practical skills. These links should explain cause and effect rather than use vague claims.

A scaffolded evidence approach makes the flowchart easier to build and assess. For each major stage, identify the input entering the stage, the approved process, the immediate output, the quality or teacher checkpoint, and one relevant impact where appropriate. Draft the sequence first, compare it with the authoritative resource, then revise any missing or unsupported links. The final evidence should include the completed flowchart and a short explanation of one feedback loop and one social or environmental impact. This proves that the student understands how Pen manufacture operates as a connected production system rather than as a loose collection of workshop activities.

  • List verified material, information, human and energy inputs.
  • Arrange the approved Pen stages in a clear production sequence.
  • Add inspection points, teacher checkpoints and feedback loops where decisions occur.
  • Identify the intended Pen and report, plus genuine waste or by-product outputs.
  • Explain at least one social impact and one environmental impact using cause and effect.
Key idea: A strong Pen production flowchart shows how verified inputs move through approved processes, checks and feedback loops to create the intended product, evidence and real production outputs.

Theory 3

Explaining social and environmental impacts of Pen production and use

Social and environmental impacts are the effects that Pen production and use can have on people, communities, resources and natural systems. The assessment expects more than a claim that timber is sustainable or that handmade products are better. Students must connect each claim to evidence from the actual project and qualify anything that has not been verified. The supplied Pen Making resource, teacher information, product labels and school procedures are the starting sources. If the origin of the timber, kit or finish is unknown, state that limitation rather than inventing a supplier, certification or environmental benefit.

Timber sourcing is one important environmental consideration, but the source and species used in this project cannot be assumed. A responsible discussion can explain that timber production may involve land use, harvesting, transport and processing, while also recognising that impacts vary according to the actual source and management practices. Project-specific evidence might include the verified stock description, information supplied by the teacher or a label attached to local stock. Efficient use of the approved blank also matters. Careful selection, accurate marking and controlled material removal can reduce avoidable waste, even when the wider sourcing details remain uncertain.

Pen manufacture also produces dust, swarf, offcuts, packaging and other waste or by-products. These outputs should be identified honestly in the production flowchart and managed through the school's approved systems. Students can distinguish between a usable offcut, recyclable material where the local system accepts it, and waste requiring disposal. The metal and plastic parts in the supplied kit also carry impacts from extraction, manufacture, packaging and transport, but their exact origin and composition should not be guessed. A balanced judgement recognises that a small product still uses several materials and that reducing loss during manufacture is one practical area students can control.

Finishing products may contribute to appearance and protection, but they also involve material use, packaging, possible emissions and controlled waste. The actual product label, Safety Data Sheet, teacher demonstration and workshop SOP determine what can be claimed and how the product is handled. Students should not describe the finish as natural, non-toxic, environmentally friendly or low-impact unless reliable product information supports that statement. A more accurate evaluation might explain that controlled application reduces unnecessary product use and that correct curing can support durability. This connects environmental responsibility with quality rather than treating them as separate topics.

Social impacts include usefulness, learning, safety and the value people place on a handmade object. The Pen can serve a practical writing purpose, develop student skill and confidence, and carry personal or gift value because the maker can explain its material, decisions and manufacture. These benefits must be balanced against the time, supervision, equipment and resources required. A handmade product is not automatically socially superior to a mass-produced one. Its value depends on safe production, suitable quality, intended use and the meaning it has for the user. Student reflections or informed user feedback can provide evidence for these claims.

Product life brings together environmental and social trade-offs. A Pen that remains useful and is cared for may avoid early replacement, but students should not claim a lifespan that has not been tested. Consider whether the product can be maintained, whether parts might be repaired or reused under approved guidance, and how timber, metal, plastic and finish could complicate disposal at the end of use. Strong evidence includes a project-specific impact table, flowchart annotations, photographs of material efficiency and waste handling, product-label information, and qualified user feedback. The final judgement should identify benefits, limitations and one realistic improvement rather than presenting the Pen as completely sustainable.

  • Separate verified project facts from assumptions about sourcing, materials and products.
  • Explain how careful blank use, controlled removal and waste sorting affect material efficiency.
  • Consider the impacts of timber, kit components, packaging and the approved finish.
  • Balance usefulness, skill development and gift value against resource use and production demands.
  • Support claims with project records, reliable product information and qualified user feedback.
Key idea: A credible impact evaluation uses project-specific evidence, states uncertainties honestly and balances the Pen's usefulness and learning value against its material, waste and end-of-life trade-offs.
